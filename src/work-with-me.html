What I'd do at your AI startup

A short read for founders thinking about hiring a community/creator lead

2 min read

"We have 5,000 users, a Discord that's getting noisy, a few power users making YouTube videos, and no one whose full-time job is to make this all add up to growth. Vishal will come in, talk to those users, build a structured program around the best ones, ship the tools to track what's working, and give us a weekly read on what the community is telling us. He's done this twice — at a startup from zero and at Google at massive scale."

That's the pitch.

What that looks like, week by week

I'd find people who already love the product.

Search YouTube, Reddit, Twitter, Discord. Make a list of the 30 most active users. DM each one. Some will ignore me. Some will become the founding ambassadors.

I'd give them reasons to do more.

Money for milestones, free credits, early access to features, their face on the company blog, intros to investors when they're raising. Different creators want different things — my job is figuring out which lever works for which person.

I'd build the place where they hang out.

A Discord, a Slack community, a forum. Set the rules. Pick the categories. Hire the moderators or do it myself. Run the weekly hangouts. Spotlight the best work.

I'd make their stuff easier to find.

If three creators made tutorials about your product, those three videos should be on the company site. If five customers wrote about a use case, those should be a curated library. Right now at most companies, this content lives outside the company's surface — I'd pull it in and organize it.

I'd send the team weekly signal.

Every week, I'd write a one-page note: what users are saying, what's broken, what's working, who's making content, what the competitors are doing. Founders pay attention to this because it tells them things they can't see from inside the building.

I'd ship small tools to make all of the above scale.

I did this at Firecrawl with TubeMonitor. Most community people stop at "we need a tool to track X" and wait for engineering. I'd build it myself with AI tooling. That's rare — most people in this role can't do this.

Why this fits Series A/B specifically

A Series A AI startup has 15–40 people. The product is working, users are growing, but the team is too small for a full marketing org. There's no one whose job is "talk to the users every day." Founders try to do it themselves but they don't have time. The community grows faster than the company can support it. Things start breaking — support gets slow, creators feel ignored, partners don't know who to talk to.

This is the moment I'd come in. I'm not hired to invent something new — I'm hired to catch the wave that's already happening before it spills.

By Series B, the company usually has 60–120 people and the community is much bigger. The job changes — I'd hire a team of 2–4 people, run the ambassador program at larger scale, and start doing more strategic work like deciding which conferences to sponsor or which YouTubers to partner with formally. I've done this transition before at Google, where I ran a 3,500-person mentor network. I know what breaks when a community goes from 200 active users to 5,000.

The five things I'm best at

  1. 1.

    Building an ambassador or creator program from zero. I did this at Firecrawl — 10 creators, 5 tiers, milestone payouts, 4x growth in referrals. I can repeat it at a new company in 6–8 weeks.

  2. 2.

    Running a developer or builder community at scale. 3,500 mentors at Google, 300 campuses at CodeChef. I know what breaks when the community grows past the founder's reach.

  3. 3.

    Turning customer signal into product feedback. At Google I built a structured pipeline that converted user complaints into Buganizer tickets engineers actually fixed.

  4. 4.

    Operating in messy, early-stage environments where structure doesn't exist. I stood up Firecrawl's entire off-page motion with no prior owner.

  5. 5.

    Shipping small internal tools when no one else will. TubeMonitor is the proof.

What I'm not the right person for

  • —Pure event management — I can run an event, but it's not my edge.
  • —Writing technical tutorials yourself — I'm a content operator, not a writer.
  • —Outbound sales / BDR work — Not my zone.
  • —Production engineering — I orchestrate AI tooling; I don't ship customer-facing code.
  • —Performance marketing or paid ads — Different muscle.
