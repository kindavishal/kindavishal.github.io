What I'd do at your AI startup

A short read for founders thinking about hiring a Developer Community Manager or Program Manager

"We have 5,000 users, a Discord that's getting noisy, a few power users making YouTube videos, and no one whose full-time job is to make this all add up to growth. Vishal will come in, talk to those users, build a structured program around the best ones, ship the tools to track what's working, and give us a weekly read on what the community is telling us. He's done this twice — at a startup from zero and at Google at massive scale."

That's the pitch.

What that looks like, week by week

I'd find people who already love the product.

Search YouTube, Reddit, Twitter, Discord. Make a list of the 30 most active users. DM each one. Some will ignore me. Some will become the founding ambassadors.

I'd give them reasons to do more.

Rewards for hitting milestones, free credits, early access to features, a spot on the company blog, warm intros when they're raising. People want different things — my job is working out what matters to each person.

I'd build the place where they hang out.

A Discord, a Slack community, a forum. Set the rules. Pick the categories. Hire the moderators or do it myself. Run the weekly hangouts. Spotlight the best work.

I'd make their work easier to find.

If three people made tutorials about your product, those videos should be on the company site. If five customers wrote about a use case, those should be a curated library. At most companies this content lives outside the company's own surface — I'd pull it in and organise it.

I'd send the team a weekly signal.

Every week I'd write a one-page note: what users are saying, what's broken, what's working, who's making content, what competitors are doing. Founders pay attention to this because it tells them things they can't see from inside the building.

I'd ship small tools to make all of it scale.

I did this at Firecrawl with TubeMonitor. Tracking tools usually need engineering time, and that time is hard to come by on a small team. I build them myself — that's the edge I bring from an engineering background.

Why this fits Series A/B

A Series A AI startup has 15–40 people. The product is working and users are growing, but the team is too small for a full marketing org. Nobody's job is "talk to the users every day." Founders try to do it themselves and run out of time. The community grows faster than the company can support it, and things start breaking — support gets slow, contributors feel ignored, partners don't know who to talk to.

This is the moment I'd come in. I'm not hired to invent something new. I'm hired to catch the wave that's already happening before it spills.

By Series B the company usually has 60–120 people and a much bigger community. The job changes — I'd hire a team of two to four, run the ambassador program at larger scale, and take on more strategic calls like which conferences to sponsor or which creators to partner with formally. I've made this transition before at Google, where I ran a 3,500-person mentor network. I know what breaks when a community goes from 200 active users to 5,000.

The five things I'm best at

  1. Building an ambassador or creator program from zero. I did this at Firecrawl — 10 creators, 4 programs, milestone rewards, 4x growth in referrals. I can repeat it at a new company in 6–8 weeks.
  2. Running a developer community at scale. 3,500 mentors at Google, 300 campuses at CodeChef. I know what breaks when a community grows past the founder's reach.
  3. Turning user signal into product feedback. At Google I built a pipeline that turned user complaints into tickets engineers actually fixed.
  4. Operating in early-stage environments where structure doesn't exist yet. I stood up Firecrawl's entire off-page motion with no prior owner.
  5. Shipping the small internal tools a team needs. TubeMonitor is the proof.

What I'm not the right person for

  • Pure event management — I can run an event, but it isn't my edge.
  • Writing the technical tutorials myself — I organise and amplify what others write.
  • Outbound sales — not my zone.
  • Production engineering — I build internal tools, but I don't ship customer-facing code.
  • Performance marketing or paid ads — a different muscle.
