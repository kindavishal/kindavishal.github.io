How I Work

Community is not a cost center. Here's the why.

Two questions are hard to answer in most community work. What did a member actually get out of this? And what did the company get back? I build programs where both are answerable, backed by data, and come from the same system.

What do members actually get?

People join for different reasons. Some want to learn, some want an audience, some want recognition for work they already do. I design rewards around what members actually value — early access, mentorship, visibility, and real support for their growth. I also make sure what the program gives back is worth what it costs, so it keeps running instead of ending with a budget cycle.

What about the attribution layer?

Showing what community work returned usually needs engineering time, and that time is hard to come by on any team. So I build it myself — Apps Script automations to collect the data, Looker Studio dashboards to make it readable. At Google that turned manual reporting into something the program could actually see itself in. At Firecrawl I shipped affiliate tracking down to each partner, and the team used it to decide payouts.

Both sides read the same numbers

This is a habit I've built over a decade — across independent communities, GitHub, CodeChef, Google, and Firecrawl. The numbers that show what developers gained are the same numbers that show leadership what the program returned, so member value and business returns stop being two separate arguments. And communities that feel unfair or unsafe stop growing. Clear rules and good moderation aren't extras. They're what keeps the numbers going up.
