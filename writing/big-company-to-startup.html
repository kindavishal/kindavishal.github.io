What changes when you move from a big company to a Series A startup

Jul 2026 ~5 min read

I spent nearly four years working across Google's India developer ecosystem. Then I joined Firecrawl — a post-Series A AI infrastructure company with about 20 people. I've been asked a few times now what changed. The honest answer is: almost everything about the daily work, and almost nothing about what makes you effective.

The feedback loop goes from quarterly to daily

At a large company, programs run on quarterly cycles. You plan in Q1, pilot in Q2, review results in Q3. You might wait months to learn whether something worked. That cycle has real benefits — it forces rigour, protects against impulsive pivots, and keeps large teams aligned.

At a startup, I launched the ambassador program and had usable results within four weeks. A creator posted a video, I could see referral data the same day, and the program structure got revised twice in the first month based on what the numbers said. That speed is intoxicating, but it's also dangerous if you don't have the discipline to separate signal from noise. A bad week at a startup can look like a failure when it's actually just variance.

The skill that transfers from big-company work isn't patience — it's knowing which data to wait for and which to act on immediately. That judgment doesn't come from the startup environment. It comes from having run programs long enough to know what early signals actually predict.

Nobody is going to build infrastructure for you

At Google, most of the infrastructure I needed already existed. There were internal tools for tracking, dashboards for reporting, established vendor workflows, legal review processes, and teams whose entire job was supporting operational functions. My work was to operate within those systems well.

At Firecrawl, when I needed a YouTube analytics tool, I built one. When I needed an ambassador tracking system, I set up the Notion database and the Typeform intake myself. When I needed SEO impression data, I built the reporting pipeline. Not because I wanted to — because there was nobody else to do it.

This is the most practical difference. If you're someone who thrives when systems exist and your job is to optimise them, startups will frustrate you. If you're someone who's been quietly annoyed that the systems weren't built the way you'd build them, startups are where that instinct becomes an advantage.

Your title means nothing, your output means everything

At a large company, your role defines your scope. You're "Operations Manager" or "Program Manager" and the boundaries of your work are mostly clear. People know what to expect from you based on the title.

At a startup, my scope on paper was "community and creator lead." In practice, I ended up owning off-page SEO, managing an external Reddit agency, building internal analytics tooling, designing affiliate economics, writing creator briefs, and doing competitive intelligence. Not because anyone asked me to — because these things needed to happen and I was the person closest to them.

If you care about role clarity, this will feel like chaos. If you care about impact, it's the best version of work I've experienced. You find the gap, you fill it, and nobody debates whether it's "your job."

Stakeholder management doesn't disappear — it just gets faster

People told me startups don't have politics. That's wrong. What startups don't have is slow politics. Decisions that would take three weeks of alignment meetings at a big company happen in a 15-minute Slack thread. But the dynamics are the same — you still need to make a case, get buy-in from people who have different priorities, and handle disagreement without burning relationships.

The skill I leaned on most from my time at Google wasn't any specific operational framework. It was knowing how to present a recommendation with enough context that the other person can say yes without needing a follow-up meeting. That skill is universal. The medium changes — from a formal doc to a Slack message — but the muscle is the same.

What doesn't change

The things that made me effective at Google are the same things that make me effective now: defining clear criteria before making decisions, documenting processes before they break, measuring leading indicators instead of lagging ones, and being willing to hold a quality bar even when it's easier not to.

These aren't big-company skills or startup skills. They're operating skills. The context changes — the weekly reporting cadence, the number of stakeholders, the speed of iteration. But the underlying discipline is portable.

If you're thinking about making the move: the hardest part isn't learning new tools or adapting to the speed. It's realising that most of what you learned at the big company is still useful — it just needs to be applied differently, at a different clock speed, with fewer guardrails. The people who struggle with the transition are usually the ones who either discard everything they learned or try to transplant it wholesale. The answer is somewhere in between.
