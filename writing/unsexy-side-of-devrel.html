The unsexy side of developer relations nobody talks about

Jul 2026 ~5 min read

If you follow DevRel discourse online, you'd think the job is mostly giving talks, writing blog posts, and hanging out in Discord servers. Those are real parts of it. But across two companies and roughly four years of ecosystem work, I'd estimate about 70% of my time was spent on things nobody posts about on LinkedIn.

This is about that 70%.

Spreadsheets that keep partnerships alive

At Google, I managed 300+ institutional partnerships and 300+ active MOUs. Each partnership had its own timeline, its own stakeholders, its own compliance requirements, and its own set of things that could go wrong. Tracking all of this required spreadsheets — not elegant dashboards, not purpose-built CRMs — spreadsheets. Google Sheets with conditional formatting, linked tabs, and Apps Script automations I wrote myself to pull data from different sources.

The romance of DevRel ends the moment you need to reconcile partner data across three systems, figure out which of 150 institutions hasn't submitted their quarterly report, and send follow-up emails that are firm enough to get a response without burning a relationship you spent six months building.

Nobody teaches you this. You learn it by having 40 things slip through the cracks in your first quarter and deciding you never want that feeling again.

Compliance and legal reviews

Some of the programs I worked on at Google involved child-safety skilling with government partners. Zero compliance incidents across the entire programme. That outcome didn't happen because we were lucky. It happened because of review processes that were thorough to the point of being tedious — document review, stakeholder sign-off chains, privacy impact assessments, and a lot of reading policies that were written for lawyers, not programme managers.

Compliance work is the most invisible high-stakes work in DevRel. When it goes well, nobody notices. When it goes wrong, it's a crisis that puts the entire programme at risk. The skill isn't being good at compliance — it's building habits that make compliance the default instead of an afterthought.

Vendor and agency management

At Firecrawl, I manage an external Reddit agency. At Google, I worked with multiple vendors across different programme tracks. Agency management is its own skill set that overlaps with DevRel but isn't part of any DevRel job description I've seen.

You need to set expectations clearly enough that the agency delivers without constant oversight, but review output closely enough to catch when quality drops. You need to give feedback that's specific enough to be actionable without micromanaging the execution. And you need to do all of this while maintaining a relationship that's professional enough to get good work out of people who have other clients competing for their attention.

The number of community and DevRel people I've met who have never managed an external vendor is surprisingly high. It's a gap, because as programmes scale, you can't do everything in-house.

The follow-up email you send for the fourth time

Institutional partnerships move slowly. A university department head takes two weeks to respond to an email. A government partner needs internal approval that takes a month. A creator you're trying to onboard ghosts you twice before responding on the third attempt.

The patience required for this work is fundamentally different from the "move fast" energy that DevRel content tends to celebrate. It's not about urgency — it's about persistence without annoyance. Knowing when to follow up, how to phrase it so it doesn't sound desperate, and when to stop trying are skills that only come from doing the work repeatedly.

I've sent hundreds of follow-up emails. The best ones are short, reference something specific the other person said, and make it easy to say yes. The worst ones are long, explain too much context, and ask the recipient to do too many things at once. This sounds obvious, but when you're managing 50 open conversations at once, the temptation to write a comprehensive email instead of a clear one is real.

Automating the boring parts

The single highest-leverage thing I did at Google was automating roughly 20 hours per week of manual work with Apps Script. Status update emails, data aggregation from partner submissions, report generation, milestone tracking — all of it was manual when I started, and most of it was automated by the time I left.

Nobody told me to do this. It wasn't in my OKRs. I did it because the alternative was spending a full day each week on work that a script could do in minutes, and I'd rather spend that time on things that actually required human judgement.

This is the unsexy side of DevRel that compounds the most. Every hour you automate is an hour you get back every week, permanently. Over four years, those Apps Script automations probably saved more than 4,000 hours of manual work across the team. Nobody talks about this because "I wrote a Google Apps Script that sends automated status emails" doesn't make a good conference talk. But it's some of the most impactful work I did.

Why this matters

If you're considering a career in DevRel, community management, or programme management, know that the job is mostly operational. The visible parts — the talks, the content, the community events — are the top 30%. The bottom 70% is tracking, follow-ups, compliance, vendor management, and process automation. The people who last in these roles are the ones who find satisfaction in the operational work, not the ones who tolerate it while waiting for the next conference.

If spreadsheets bore you, this might not be your field. If spreadsheets bore you but you build automations to avoid them, you'll do very well.
