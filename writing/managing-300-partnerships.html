Five things I learned managing 300+ institutional partnerships

Jun 2026 ~6 min read

Between 2022 and 2025, I worked across Google's India developer ecosystem, helping scale institutional partnerships from around 100 to over 300. These were universities, training institutions, and government education bodies — each with their own stakeholders, timelines, compliance requirements, and expectations of what "partnering with Google" meant.

Managing that many partnerships taught me things I couldn't have learned at a smaller scale. Here are the five that stuck.

1. Relationships don't scale. Systems do.

When you have 20 partnerships, you can manage them on relationships. You remember who the dean is, what they care about, when their term ends, and what they said in the last meeting. You carry all of this in your head and it works.

When you have 200, you can't. You forget which institution is in the pilot phase and which has already signed the MOU. You miss a follow-up because you thought you'd remember and didn't. Two partners get conflicting information because you briefed one on Monday and the other on Friday and the situation changed in between.

The fix isn't "try harder to remember." The fix is building systems that don't require you to remember — documented partner profiles, standardised onboarding flows, milestone trackers with automated reminders, and templated communications that ensure consistency without requiring you to draft every email from scratch.

This is obvious in retrospect. It wasn't obvious in month three when my tracking system was a spreadsheet with 40 columns and I was confident I could hold it all together manually. I couldn't. Nobody can.

2. The MOU is the beginning, not the end

There's a tendency in partnership roles to treat the signed agreement as the milestone. The partner signed — celebration, update the tracker, move on to the next one. In practice, signing is the moment the real work starts.

An MOU that doesn't translate into action within 60 days is functionally dead. The partner's internal champion loses momentum, the faculty who were excited move on to other priorities, and the institutional memory of why they signed fades. I learned to front-load the first 60 days with specific, low-effort activation steps: a kickoff call within the first week, the first training cohort scheduled within 30 days, and a visible quick win within 45 days.

Across 300+ MOUs, the partnerships that lasted were the ones that activated quickly. The ones that stalled were almost always the ones where I let the gap between signing and activation stretch past two months.

3. Your champion will leave. Plan for it.

Every institutional partnership has a champion — the person inside the partner organisation who pushed for the partnership, who answers your emails, who makes things happen internally. In academic institutions, these are usually department heads or programme coordinators.

They leave. They rotate to a different department, they retire, they take a position at another university. When that happens, the partnership doesn't automatically die — but it enters a critical period where the new person either picks it up or lets it lapse.

The insurance against this is documentation. If the partnership's history, goals, deliverables, and current status are documented in a format that a new stakeholder can read in 15 minutes, the transition works. If the partnership exists only in email threads between you and the old champion, it probably doesn't survive the transition.

I started building "partner handoff docs" after losing two partnerships to champion turnover in the same quarter. Each doc covered: why the partner joined, what's been delivered, what's pending, who the key contacts are, and what the next milestone is. These took 30 minutes to write and saved months of relationship rebuilding.

4. Consistency beats intensity

The instinct when managing a lot of partnerships is to give the most attention to the ones that need the most help. The institution that's struggling to run their first training session gets extra calls, extra support, extra time. Meanwhile, the institution that's running smoothly gets ignored because they don't need you.

This is a mistake. The smoothly running partnerships need regular contact — not crisis-level attention, but a monthly check-in, a quarterly review, an acknowledgement that their work matters. Without it, even strong partnerships quietly disengage. The champion stops responding as quickly. The reporting gets late. The next cohort is a little smaller. Nothing dramatic — just a slow fade that's hard to notice until it's advanced.

I built a simple cadence: every partner got at least one touchpoint per month, regardless of status. For struggling partners, that touchpoint was hands-on support. For thriving partners, it was a 15-minute status call and a thank-you. The volume of touchpoints was high, but each individual one was short. Consistency across 300 partners is a scheduling problem, not a capacity problem, if you keep each interaction focused.

5. The best partnerships produce things you didn't plan for

The most valuable outcomes from the partnership programme were often things I didn't anticipate when we designed it. A university partner produced a curriculum adaptation that other partners adopted. A group of mentors from the educator community built a peer support network that reduced the support load on our team. A government partner's compliance requirements forced us to build a review process that made the entire programme more rigorous.

These emergent outcomes don't happen when you over-specify what the partnership should produce. They happen when you define the goals clearly but leave room for the partner to bring their own strengths to the table. The best partners aren't the ones who follow your playbook perfectly — they're the ones who understand the goal well enough to find a better path than the one you planned.

This is also why the "partner as vendor" framing fails. Vendors execute a scope of work. Partners co-create outcomes. If you treat institutional partners like vendors — specifying deliverables, tracking SLAs, managing performance against contract terms — you get compliance but not creativity. The 300K+ learners trained and 1,000+ Android apps deployed through the network didn't come from specification. They came from partners who understood the goal and ran with it.

The short version

  1. Build systems before you need them — your memory doesn't scale
  2. Activate fast after signing — 60 days or the partnership stalls
  3. Document for champion turnover — it will happen
  4. Touch every partner monthly, even the ones that don't need you
  5. Define goals clearly but leave room for partners to surprise you

Most of these lessons sound obvious. They are. The hard part isn't knowing them — it's building the discipline to follow them consistently when you're managing 300 relationships at once and each one has its own timeline, its own stakeholders, and its own version of urgency. The lessons that stick aren't the clever ones. They're the ones where the cost of ignoring them was high enough that you never forget.
