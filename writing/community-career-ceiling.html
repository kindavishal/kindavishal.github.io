Leaving community doubles your odds of getting promoted

August 2026 ~5 min read

Richard Millington at FeverBee did something most people writing about careers never bother with. He followed the same people over time.

He took 86 community professionals from a 2015 event, and eleven years later checked where they ended up.

Of the people who stayed in community, 28% reached Director or VP. Of the people who moved into another function, 60% did. Leaving roughly doubled the odds of climbing.

The number I keep coming back to is a different one. 24% were still in the same role. Same job, eleven years later.

The shape of the problem

Most career advice here only counts the people it worked for. Someone made it to VP of Community, wrote about how, and everyone read it as a map. Nobody counts the people who followed the same route and are still where they started.

One number explains it: roughly 21 managers for every one VP in community. That pyramid is far flatter at the top than most professions. There simply isn't much to climb into.

So the honest question is not "am I good enough to get promoted." It's "does this field have anywhere to put me." For a lot of people the answer is no. Doing the work well does not change it.

Only 8% of those 86 people climbed within community. 41% moved sideways into a different function. 20% went independent.

Why I think the gap exists

Here's where I'll add something rather than just repeat the data.

The two things that reliably precede a senior title anywhere are profit-line responsibility and managing people. Community roles almost never come with the first one.

Not because the work doesn't create value. Because the value lands in someone else's numbers. The tutorial a community member wrote shows up in organic acquisition. The bug an ambassador reported shows up in product quality. The developer who stayed because someone answered their question shows up in retention. Every one of those is real, and every one of them gets counted by a different team.

A function whose returns are collected by other functions cannot build a case for its own seniority. That's not a measurement inconvenience. It's the whole ceiling.

So I don't think the 28% versus 60% gap measures whether community is a bad career. It measures how many people could point at a number and say that one is mine.

What actually moves it

Millington lists three paths that work. Get to an organisation big enough to have a real team, and manage one by year four. Work somewhere community is the product, like Discourse or Reddit. Or go independent. All three are about changing your context rather than your performance.

I'd add a fourth, and it's the one I've spent my career on.

Build the thing that assigns the return to your programme. If the value keeps landing in other people's numbers, instrument it so it doesn't. That means a data model connecting a community action to a business result, one person at a time.

I've written up exactly how I do that — four tables, Apps Script and Looker Studio, no engineering time required. At Firecrawl that layer is what the team used to decide partner payouts. It turned "community probably helped" into a per-partner number people argued about on the merits.

The honest read on the data

  • Staying: 28% reached Director or VP over 11 years.
  • Leaving for another function: 60%.
  • Still in the same role after 11 years: 24%.
  • Climbed within community itself: 8%.
  • Roughly 21 managers per VP. There is very little above you.

Where I'd push back, and where I wouldn't

I won't push back on the data. It's the most honest thing anyone has published about this field. It follows the same people over time instead of surveying different ones each year, which is why it beats the advice it contradicts.

What I'd question is the conclusion people will draw from it. The lesson is not "leave community." It's that being good at community work is not a promotion strategy, and it never was. The people who climbed did something different. They got somewhere with a real team, or somewhere community was the business, or they built their case in numbers a CFO recognises.

I'd also name the obvious confounder, which Millington names himself: people who switch functions may just be more ambitious to begin with. The gap is real. The causation is murkier than the number makes it look.

What I'd tell someone three years in

Ask one question about your current role. Can this job ever produce a number the business already cares about?

If yes, go build it, and make sure your name is on it.

If no — if the returns will always be collected by product, marketing or sales, and you'll always be arguing from anecdote — the ceiling is in the role, not in you. Eleven years is a long time to find that out by accident.

The version of this that decides budgets, not just careers, is why DevRel gets cut first.
