Why your community program is a cost center (and how to change that)

Jun 2026 ~5 min read

Here's a pattern I've seen at three different companies. Someone starts a community program. It grows. Members are active. Content gets produced. The founder or VP asks: "What's the ROI?" The community manager shows Discord member count, event attendance, and maybe a few testimonials. The founder nods politely and privately wonders whether the headcount could be better spent on paid acquisition.

The community manager isn't wrong — those are real outputs. The problem is that they're the wrong metrics for the conversation being had. The founder is asking a revenue question and getting an engagement answer.

The lagging metrics trap

Most community programs report lagging metrics: signups attributed to community, revenue from community-sourced users, NPS from community members. These metrics are real, but they have a fatal flaw — they tell you what already happened. By the time you see them, it's too late to change the outcome.

Worse, lagging metrics in community programmes tend to be noisy. Attribution is messy. A user who found the product through a creator's YouTube video, then signed up two weeks later from a Google search, then converted after reading a docs page — who gets credit? The creator? SEO? Product? Community programmes routinely under-count their own contribution because the attribution chain is long and multi-touch.

When you report metrics that are both lagging and noisy, you hand leadership a reason to deprioritise the program. Not because the program isn't working — because the numbers can't prove that it is.

Switch to leading indicators

At Firecrawl, I reframed the ambassador programme KPIs from creator-attributed signups to SEO and GEO surface area. The specific leading indicators:

  • YouTube search impressions — how many times creator content appeared in YouTube search results for target keywords
  • Google appearances — whether creator tutorials ranked on Google for product-adjacent queries
  • AI search citations — whether the product appeared in AI-generated search responses, and which creator content was cited
  • Content publish cadence — number of creator-produced pieces per week that met quality bar
  • Competitive share of voice — our mentions vs competitor mentions across the same creator channels

The difference isn't just measurement — it's actionability. If YouTube search impressions drop this week, I know what to do next week: review the keyword targets, adjust the creator brief, check whether recent content was indexed correctly. If signups are down this month, I have no idea which lever to pull.

The compound interest argument

Paid acquisition is linear. You spend $10K on ads, you get X signups. You stop spending, the signups stop. Community content is compounding. A creator's tutorial published in January is still ranking in July. A well-answered question on Reddit is still pulling search traffic six months later. A YouTube integration video that ranks for "how to scrape websites with AI" will generate leads for years.

The problem is that compound curves look flat at the beginning. In month one, the creator programme looks like it's producing a few videos that got a few thousand views. In month six, those same videos have accumulated hundreds of thousands of views and the newer videos are launching into an existing surface area. The returns look bad early and good later — which is exactly the opposite of how paid acquisition looks.

If you report lagging metrics, you're asking leadership to evaluate a compound curve using point-in-time data. It will always look worse than paid acquisition until it doesn't. By the time it doesn't, the program has usually been cut.

How to make the case internally

Three things that have worked for me:

Frame community as a distribution channel, not a support function. The words matter. "Community programme" sounds like a cost centre. "Organic distribution through technical creators" sounds like a growth channel. You're doing the same work either way, but the framing determines which budget conversation you're part of.

Report leading indicators alongside lagging ones, never instead of them. Leadership wants to see signups. Show them. But put the leading indicators first and explain the causal chain: "YouTube impressions are up 40% week-over-week, which means these creator videos are ranking. Historically, ranking videos drive X% of organic trial starts within 30 days." You're giving them a story with a predictable ending instead of a number with no context.

Show the counterfactual cost. What would it cost to produce the same number of indexed pages, YouTube videos, and search impressions through paid means? When the answer is "several multiples of the program budget," the ROI conversation changes. Community isn't free — you're paying creator milestones and your own salary — but it's dramatically cheaper per impression than the alternative.

The real problem is positioning, not performance

Most community programmes that get cut aren't underperforming. They're under-positioned. The person running the programme is doing good work but reporting it in a way that makes it easy to deprioritise. If you treat community as a growth channel and report accordingly, the conversation with leadership sounds fundamentally different.

This isn't about spin. It's about measuring the right things. Leading indicators are genuinely more useful for day-to-day decision-making than lagging ones. The fact that they also make the programme look better to leadership is a side effect of measuring correctly, not a trick.
