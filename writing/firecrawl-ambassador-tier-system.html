How I designed the Firecrawl ambassador tier system

May 2026 ~6 min read

When I joined Firecrawl post-Series A, the company had no creator program. There were a handful of creators making content organically, a few intro emails sitting unanswered, and zero structure for what "working with creators" actually meant. My job was to build the whole thing from scratch.

This is the design playbook I ran to get from zero to ten ambassadors in four weeks — and four times the referral volume in the months that followed.

The problem with most creator programs

Most creator programs fail not because the company lacks good creators — they fail because the program never defines what it's selecting for. You end up with vague application criteria, inconsistent outreach, and a roster of "ambassadors" who don't know what they're supposed to do or why they were chosen.

The design problem I was solving wasn't "how do we get creators to mention Firecrawl." It was: how do we build a program with real classification criteria, real economics, and real incentive alignment — one that gets stronger as it scales instead of harder to manage?

The five-tier structure

I designed five tiers, each with explicit entry criteria. Not gut-feel criteria — measurable ones: subscriber count, view velocity, technical depth of existing content, and demonstrated integration quality.

The tier structure serves two purposes. First, it gives a consistent way to classify inbound applicants without making a judgment call each time. Second, it signals to creators exactly what they need to do to move up — which means the program incentivises improvement without you having to ask for it.

The upper tiers required demonstrated technical integration with Firecrawl's API, not just a mention in a video. To qualify, you needed to build something with the product and document it on camera. If you had an audience but no integration depth, you could still enter at a lower tier and work your way up. The structure preserved quality at the top without blocking early-stage creators from participating.

Written criteria also made the hard conversations easier. When a creator didn't meet the bar, I had a document to point to — not a judgment call to defend.

The economics: milestone-based, not performance-based

The most important design decision was milestone-based payouts over per-view or per-signup payouts.

Per-view payouts optimize for views. Per-signup payouts optimize for conversion spam. Neither is what I wanted. I wanted creators building durable, discoverable content — tutorials that answer real technical questions, rank on YouTube search, and compound over time. You can't build that on an incentive that rewards upload volume or thumbnail CTR.

The milestone structure ran from $625 to $11,250 per milestone, graduated across tiers. Each milestone was tied to a deliverable — the integration video, the follow-up tutorial, the deep-dive — plus a minimum performance threshold. Hitting the threshold without the deliverable didn't trigger the payout.

Program economics at a glance

  • Milestone range: $625–$11,250 per milestone, graduated by tier
  • Streak system: consecutive completions earn progressively higher payouts
  • Each milestone: content deliverable + minimum performance threshold
  • Payout trigger: manual review against criteria, not automated on hit-count

On top of the base milestone structure, I layered a streak system — consecutive milestone completions earned progressively higher payouts. The goal was retention, not acquisition. Signing an ambassador is cheap. Keeping one producing quality content for six months is the actual work.

The ops stack

The full pipeline runs on Typeform → Notion → Slack with no enterprise SaaS overhead. Typeform handles intake. Notion is the source of truth for every ambassador: tier, milestone status, payout history, content links. Slack is where live updates go and where the creator-facing community exists.

I also built TubeMonitor — a custom Next.js + Supabase app on Firecrawl's own scraping primitives — to track view performance, spillover, engagement signals, and competitive share of voice across the creator network. That's a separate post, but the point is: don't rely on third-party YouTube analytics tools when you're building on a scraping product.

Recruitment: 10 in 4 weeks

I sourced the first cohort manually. No public launch, no "apply here" announcement blast. I identified creators who had already mentioned Firecrawl or adjacent tools, assessed their technical depth from existing content, and sent short, direct outreach that was specific to something they'd already built.

Ten creators across five tiers in four weeks. One early integration drove 60K views in week one. That result was the proof-of-concept I needed to show the team that the structure worked — and it demonstrated what differentiated this from a basic affiliate link program: a creator who genuinely understood the product produced content that ranked, converted, and compounded.

Results

Creator-attributed referrals went from 1.7K to 7.2K weekly — a 4x increase. The growth wasn't from more creators. It was from better-quality content by well-briefed creators who actually understood what they were integrating. Volume without quality would have flatlined the referral numbers and produced content nobody watched past the first minute.

What I'd do differently

Two things, both operational.

Automate milestone review earlier. When you have tight criteria, every payout requires a judgment call. I reviewed each milestone ad hoc — workable at ten ambassadors, a bottleneck at scale. I'd build a structured review template populated with TubeMonitor data from week one instead of retrofitting it after the first scaling friction.

Front-load the creator brief. The first cohort produced lower-quality drafts because the brief wasn't specific enough about what a good integration looked like. We iterated on it over three feedback cycles. That cost two weeks I could have recovered upfront.

The brief is the most important artefact in the program. It's the difference between a creator who produces a tutorial worth watching and one who produces a feature mention nobody clicks. Write it before you recruit anyone.
